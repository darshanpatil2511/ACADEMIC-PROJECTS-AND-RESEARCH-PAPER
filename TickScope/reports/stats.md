## 3. Does order-flow imbalance predict the next minute's return?

Imbalance(t) = (taker buy volume − taker sell volume) / total volume in minute t. Regression: return(t+1) = a + b · imbalance(t) + e.

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Pairs (n) | 10,079 |
| Pearson r | 0.0208 |
| Slope b | 2.437e-05 |
| t-stat, naive OLS | 2.09 |
| t-stat, Newey-West HAC (11 lags) | 2.47 |

r = 0.0208 means imbalance explains 0.04% of the variance of the next minute's return. The relationship is statistically significant at 5% even with HAC errors (|t| = 2.47 > 1.96), with sign positive: buying pressure is followed by slightly higher prices. Note the HAC t-stat is larger than the naive one here: Newey-West corrects in both directions, and when the products (x − x̄)·e are negatively autocorrelated the long-run variance is smaller than the naive estimate. Even when significant, an effect this small (a fraction of a basis point per minute) is unlikely to survive trading costs at a one-minute horizon; order-flow effects are generally strongest at much shorter horizons, which 1-minute bars average away.

### Effective spread, realized spread and price impact (quantity-weighted, bp)

Reference price = last-trade price path (no quote data): M0 = previous second, Mh = h seconds later. Effective = 2d(P − M0)/M0, realized = 2d(P − Mh)/M0, impact = effective − realized. The realized spread is what a market maker keeps after the price moves; impact is what it loses to informed flow (adverse selection).

| 60s | real_ioc | 3,430,804 | -0.327 | -2.728 | 2.401 |

| 60s | synthetic_limit | 1,663,582 | -1.797 | -2.718 | 0.921 |

**Reading these numbers.** Negative effective spread for real_ioc and synthetic_limit means those takers traded at *better* than the pre-trade reference price: the synthetic makers quote around the previous second's price, so when the market moves their quotes are stale and incoming flow picks them off. Price impact is positive for every taker type: after a fill, the price keeps moving in the taker's direction, so the makers on the other side lose (adverse selection). A real market maker would widen quotes or update them faster than once per second to avoid this.
